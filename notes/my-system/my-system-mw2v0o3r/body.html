When I was 13, I thought I could ‘get ahead’ of my peers with the right plan.


In my head, if I just figured out exactly how to study better than the smartest kid, talk better than the class-clown, and find the best productivity system, I’d ascend to become a hyper-successful, popular Ubermensch.


I think I got addicted to planning - it would fill me up with a fuzzy, warm feeling, like I was figuring out how to conquer the world.


Funnily enough, whenever it was time to implement one of these plans, anxiety would well up in my chest, and I’d procrastinate on it. I’d enter a ‘fall’, where I wouldn’t do anything productive, then a ‘rise’, where - you guessed it - I made another plan.


Obviously, I didn’t succeed.


At all.


Regardless of how well I planned, I hadn’t learned how to approach difficult problems and situations or tough through a hard time.


So while I tried to fill my time with planning - for it was the only thing that brought me genuine comfort - other kids practiced shooting hoops, solving math problems, and talking to people. A thousand times. Imperfectly, and in-optimally.


Ironically, those were the same kids that triggered my insecurity in middle school.
