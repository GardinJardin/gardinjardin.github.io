• For the past month, I’ve been working on a project to analyze extra headroom in ERCOT, Texas’s grid operator, to see how many extra data-centers could fit. 


I first engaged with the problem when I read a Duke paper that calculated that ERCOT could host around 10 GW of extra headroom if large loads like data centers agreed to have their energy curtailed around 0.5% of the time.


But the strategy they chose seemed pretty simplistic to me. They calculated how much extra energy was available in past years by seeing how much energy wasn’t used, that could have been used.


This raises a series of problems: both demand and supply for grid power has shifted since the historical year they used. I added future forecasting for ERCOT and used a more rigorous method to calculate peak grid capacity. Wrestling with datasets and trying to minimize unreasonable assumptions was a case study on how difficult proper data science is, and how much depends on high quality data.


The loop I use to demolish hard problems is: research, hypothesizing, predicting, creating a prototype, and rapidly breaking that prototype under objective market feedback. Thus, I reached out to around 20-30 energy professionals: mainly journalists, professors, regulators to see if they’d find such a tool useful. The main piece of feedback I got was that transmission needed to be factored in to establish more accurate numbers. I’ve been building a region-by-region model and trying to figure out how to implement line flow models for the past 7 days.
