Substack essay on my project:

My findings on the potential of flexible-connect deals for ERCOT, based on Duke’s Rethinking Load Growth paper.

Let me explain how I reached that number.

The problem, and the solution

Around 2 weeks ago, I read a super interesting paper by Duke, which argued that many of the problems around the US grid having ‘no extra space’ left was tied to the grid’s tendency to overestimate frequent demand.

When Johnny Industrialist asks the grid to connect to his data center, the grid looks at its most intense times - like Winter Storm Uri, or right after Will Smith slapped Chris Rock - and decides whether new load could be supported during those times. If it can’t, Johnny goes home disappointed. Studies estimate that US grids run at roughly half their peak capacity on average, making growth dependent on capacity expansion, instead of a more frugal use of resources we already possess.

So, what was Duke’s solution?

We could allow data centers - pretty much any large energy consumer - to connect to the grid without waiting for heavy investments in infrastructure, if they agreed to turn off for x hours every year.

Right now, there are thousands of proposed projects, and most of them are going to be stuck in waiting-line limbo forever, without ever getting approval. This relatively simple solution could allow projects to connect early, and connect quick.

Duke calculated how much power flexible-connect could open up for ERCOT(Texas’s statewide operator and authority), which was around 10 GW. To put that into perspective, an average hyperscale AI datacenter is 100 - 300 MW, with some running up to 1 GW. ERCOT's record summer demand is ~85.5 GW, so 10 GW would be 12% of the most power Texas has ever used at once.

So, how did Duke find this extra power?


They used a fancy technique called root-finding, and applied it to historical data.

First, they took the past 9 years of hourly demand data, harvested from official sources.

They chose the highest summer and winter peaks from all 9 years. The reason they did this is because that gives them a quick way to determine the floor on available grid capacity - if ‘x GW’ is the highest power the grid’s ever served, they can use that as a stand-in for the grid’s actual peak capacity, while calculating the actual capacity bottom-up.

Then, they chose a specific curtailment rate: basically, if you wanted to add ‘y GW’ to the grid under the condition that it disconnects for a few hours every year, what percent of MWh would be curtailed? How much energy are they losing from flexible-connection? This allows load operators to choose a rate they’d be comfortable with.

Now that they had a curtailment rate and a stand-in for peak grid capacity, they ran an algorithm over a year of historical hourly demand data - let’s say 2024 - with the goal being to find the amount of extra load that could be added to the grid with that specific curtailment rate. They chose 0.5% curtailment - manageable for a load.

How did the algorithm work?

(skip if you don’t care)

- For each hour, calculate the difference between the peak grid capacity and that hour’s stated demand. The difference is how much extra energy that hour could’ve supported. Label it ‘gap’, which is, at minimum, 0 GW, and at maximum, the peak grid capacity number.

$$\mathrm{gap}_t = \max(0,\ C_t - D_t) \qquad 0 \le \mathrm{gap}_t \le C_t$$

- First, choose a random extra load number, L, and pass into rate(L).

- For each hour, take ‘L - gap’. If the difference is positive, that means that L’s too big for that hour. If the difference is negative, L’s small enough. max(0, L − gap) gives you ‘zero’ is L fits, and the exact power gap if it doesn’t.

$$\max(0,\ L - \mathrm{gap}_t) \quad \begin{cases} 0 & L \le \mathrm{gap}_t \ \text{(fits)} \\ L - \mathrm{gap}_t & L > \mathrm{gap}_t \ \text{(too big)} \end{cases}$$

- Sum max(0, L − gap) over every hour in the year, and multiply by ‘hour’ - this gives you the exact amount of energy that would be curtailed. Divide by L x 8760 - the energy a non-flexible load of that size would harvest.

$$E_{\text{cut}} = \sum_{t=1}^{8760} \max(0,\ L - \mathrm{gap}_t) \times 1\,\mathrm{h} \qquad \mathrm{rate}(L) = \frac{E_{\text{cut}}}{L \times 8760\,\mathrm{h}}$$

- rate(L) has just given us the curtailment rate for that specific L. Now all we need to do is find the right L for our curtailment rate.

$$\mathrm{rate}(L) = r \;\Rightarrow\; \text{solve for } L$$

- f(L) = rate(L) − 0.005. f(L) is positive when rate(L) is bigger than our preferred curtailment rate, and it’s negative when rate(L) is smaller. We just need to find the rate that makes f(L) = 0.

$$f(L) = \mathrm{rate}(L) - 0.005 \qquad f(L^*) = 0$$

- The fortunate part of this algorithm is that f(L) is monotonic - that means that it always increases, so it’s only going to cross 0 once(this makes sense - as the load you want to accommodate increases, the rate of curtailment also increases).

$$L_1 < L_2 \Rightarrow f(L_1) \le f(L_2)$$

- At 1 MW, almost every hour has room, so the rate is about 0 and f is negative. At 100 GW, most of the load gets cut, so f is positive. f(L) is zero somewhere in between.

$$f(1\,\mathrm{MW}) < 0 < f(100{,}000\,\mathrm{MW}) \;\Rightarrow\; L^* \in (1,\ 100{,}000)\,\mathrm{MW}$$

- The root-finding algorithm takes the midpoint of these two values and sees if it’s positive or negative. If it’s positive, it takes the bottom half of values, and if it’s negative, it takes the top half.

$$m = \frac{a+b}{2} \qquad f(m) > 0 \Rightarrow b \leftarrow m \qquad f(m) < 0 \Rightarrow a \leftarrow m$$

- It repeats that step over the half, repetitively halving the range of values until - you guessed it - it gets close enough to 0. You could keep going until you reach the level of precision you want.

- What we’ve just done is calculated the exact amount of extra load we can fit with our chosen curtailment rate.

$$L^* = \text{headroom at } r = 0.5\% \quad (\text{ERCOT 2016--2024: } L^* \approx 9.99\,\mathrm{GW})$$

My findings

I decided to replicate their result for ERCOT. I got 9.99 GW.

Here’s the problem - Duke averaged their results for 2016 - 2024, but AI’s turned the national energy market upside down since 2022. I decided to average over 2020 - 2024, and I got 6.96 GW.

Because I got curious, I decided to look at each year individually: headroom barely increased between 2016-2017, but rapidly collapsed by 2022-2023.

Now, I wanted to look at the future of this deal:

- I took ERCOT’s hourly forecast as a prediction of forward demand

- I then calc-timated future supply: take the old peak capacity, and for each future year add new plants minus retirements, by GW. Do this individually for each fuel, because solar/wind have specific percent efficiencies over different hours of the day, days of the month, and months of the year.

- As the program goes hour by hour, it adds additions and subtracts retirements, each part multiplied by its efficiency rating for that specific hour.

So, what did the numbers come out to?

… It predicted zero headroom for 2027.

At first I panicked. Was Texas screwed?

No, fortunately.

I realized that I was using EIA’s supply forecast and ERCOT’s demand forecast. The key difference between these two datasets is that ERCOT’s forecast factored in approximately 48 GW of large loads who requested to join, but hadn’t joined yet. Since I was trying to find room for future data centers, it didn’t make sense to include future data centers in the baseline demand forecast.

I subtracted the extra load from the forecast and used that difference as the baseline demand. The normal grid peak was within the range of ERCOT’s own CDR prediction, meaning I could trust the numbers.

Here’s what I got:

At 0.5% curtailment, by 2030, we could add 12.85 GW. At 5%, there’s 21.6 GW of headroom.

Considering that 48 GW of data centers are already trying to join by then, the grid is still in no shape to support America’s appetite for AI.

However, this does still illustrate the potential of flexible connect deals.

Assumptions and Takeaways

- Duke took a past year’s demand and supply, which would both be underestimates, in different ways. Duke also used the historical peak demand as the peak cap, which wasn’t necessarily the actual peak capacity of the grid. Since I added future capacity additions to this number, Duke and I both might be underestimating the grid.

- ERCOT’s future hourly forecast averaged the past ~17 years of weather data, meaning that future climate trends and extreme weather events are both ignored

- ERCOT’s future hourly forecast ignored efficiency gains, which would overestimate baseline demand

- All new, requested big loads are treated as flexible-eligible

- As a professor told me, I didn’t factor in transmission or regional peak capacity. Since each transmission line has a different capacity, the region-level peak capacity could be very different from the statewide number.

- I used EIA for supply and ERCOT for demand, so even with the 48 GW being subtracted, there could still be a mismatch. V1 assumes that each agency is the best source for its specific forecast.

For V2(which I’m building right now), the simple fix to the last assumption would be to conduct the analysis for each region, individually. This should give a more accurate answer, but it’s still bound to fall short due to a lack of transmission analysis. I’m planning on conducting a line-flow analysis, but this would use synthetic datasets.

As I performed this exercise, the limitation of data was a constant annoyance. I’ve gained a newfound appreciation for data analysts: finding good data is the most important step for any predictive model. I’ve had to accept a number of different assumptions and limitations to ship the model. This problem only grew with v2: zone-by-zone data was an order of magnitude harder to calculate than ERCOT totals, forcing me to rely on plant-by-plant forecasts, rather than an official source’s ‘convenient’ estimate.
